1e&a

Before the band comes in, the drummer counts under their breath: one e and a, two e and a. The 1 is the landing, the beat everyone hears. The e, the and and the a are the quiet spaces in between, where a song gets its swing. This project takes its name from that count.

In time

Music plays in time, and your body keeps it. Hear a beat and you're already guessing the next one, leaning in, landing on it. Researchers call this entrainment. What follows pulls it apart: one sound, then a pattern, then a pulse, then something you can move to.

Reading the dots

Read the dots left to right, like a song. The space between them is time, so close dots come fast. Higher dots are higher notes, bigger dots hit harder, and a line means the note rings on.

Rhythm

Rhythm is the pattern of sounds and the gaps between them. Sing "happy and you know it, clap your hands" and you already know where the two claps go. You've heard it so often that the gap tells you.

Pitch + Intensity

Pitch is how high or low a note sits. Intensity is how hard it hits. "We wish you a merry Christmas" climbs a step every time it comes round, and the room sings it louder each time.

Beat + Meter

The beat is the steady pulse your foot finds on its own. Meter is how those beats are grouped. Happy Birthday sways in threes, Jingle Bells marches in fours like most pop, and the first beat of every group lands heaviest.

Tempo

Tempo is how fast the beat moves, counted in beats per minute. Sing "jingle bells" slowly, then quickly. The words and the pattern stay put, and only the gaps shrink. Most pop sits between 100 and 130 BPM.

Groove

Groove is what gets your body moving before you decide to. Put on Waka Waka and by the chorus everyone is bouncing on the quick syllables and jumping on the last two. Nobody tells them to. The beat does.

Syncopation

Syncopation moves the hit off the beat, into the space between the counts. Tap your foot to "1 and 2 and 3 and 4" and clap only on the "and". Your foot holds the beat, your clap slips into the gaps, and that small push is what makes music feel alive.

Everything here comes from three papers. Levitin, Grahn and London mapped how a listener locks onto a beat and starts predicting the next one. Fitch argued that music and dance grew up as one thing, which is why rhythm makes more sense when you move. And a 2022 study measured who feels groove the most, and found it was people with a good ear and people who dance.
